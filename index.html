What This Series Means by Optical Computing

Light doing part of a neural network's arithmetic: multiplying, summing or transforming values encoded in an optical field, with electronics around it to convert data in and out, apply nonlinearities and store results. Most of a neural network's operations are matrix multiplications, and a matrix–vector product is exactly what interference and propagation compute for free, so that is where almost every approach aims.

In scope

Optical matrix multiplication, optical convolution and transforms, diffractive networks, and the precision, energy and scale arguments around them. Decks 02 and 03 of this series.

Already covered

Fourier optics for inference and training: Fourier Optics for Inference and for Training. The devices: Silicon Photonics Fundamentals. Linked, not repeated.

Out of scope

Optics that only moves bits between chips. That is interconnect, the subject of Optical Technology for Data Centres (slide 12).

Reviews: Wetzstein et al., “Inference in artificial intelligence with deep optics and photonics” (Nature 588, 39, 2020); Shastri et al., “Photonics for artificial intelligence and neuromorphic computing” (arXiv:2011.00111; Nature Photonics 15, 102, 2021); McMahon, “The physics of optical computing” (Nat. Rev. Phys. 5, 717, 2023).

02

Three Physical Facts

1. Interference sums

Coherent fields add as complex amplitudes. Split an input, scale and phase-shift the copies, and recombine them: the output field is a weighted sum. A grid of such operations is a matrix–vector product (coherent and incoherent light).

2. Propagation is linear

Passing through a lens, a diffractive layer or a waveguide mesh applies a fixed linear transform to the whole field at once. A lens's is the Fourier transform (the lens as a Fourier transformer).

3. Detection is square-law

Photodiodes measure power, |E|2, losing sign and phase unless a reference beam is mixed in. Detection also multiplies, which photoelectric multiplication exploits (the phase problem; slide 09).

  • What these give: linear operations that cost no energy inside the optics, at the speed light crosses the device, on many values in parallel.
  • What they do not: nonlinearity (slide 10), memory, exact arithmetic, or a free way to get numbers in and out. McMahon's review is blunt that the speed of light itself is “not a key differentiating physical property of optics for computing” and lists eleven features of optics a design can exploit instead (arXiv:2308.00088).

04

Free-Space and Fourier Optics

A lens computes a two-dimensional Fourier transform in one pass, and a 4f system with a mask in the Fourier plane computes a convolution. The physics, the spatial light modulators that write data and masks, the precision and the conversion energy are worked through in FOptInf 01, Fourier Optics for Engineers, and are not repeated here.

  • What that series found for LLMs: standard transformer decoders contain no Fourier transforms, and even FFT-mixing architectures spend a small share of their FLOPs on transforms, so a Fourier engine's Amdahl bound is small (FOptInf 02, the Amdahl analysis; optical share). Training adds a backward pass but not a better share (FOptTrn 01).
  • The distinction that matters: a Fourier engine accelerates only transforms and convolutions; an optical matrix multiplier accelerates every matmul (transform engine against optical MAC). This series is mostly about the second.
  • Where free-space optics still leads is in sheer parallelism: a megapixel spatial light modulator holds a million values at once. The cost is frame rate: liquid-crystal devices settle in milliseconds (spatial light modulators).
05

Diffractive Networks

A diffractive network is a stack of thin layers, each a grid of pixels that delays or attenuates the light passing through. Free-space propagation between layers connects every pixel to many in the next layer, so the stack is a fixed, trained linear network that computes as light passes (diffractive deep neural networks).

  • Weakness: fixed or slowly reconfigured weights, and a linear operation per pass. The headline efficiency figures count operations as each paper defines them; compare them with care (deck 04 on reading claims).
06

Coherent Mach–Zehnder Meshes

A Mach–Zehnder interferometer with two phase shifters is a programmable 2 × 2 unitary. Arranged in a triangle (Reck) or a rectangle (Clements), N(N−1)/2 of them implement any N × N unitary; two meshes and a row of attenuators implement any matrix by its singular value decomposition (MZI meshes; devices in OptSiP 01, slide 08).

  • The demonstration that started the modern field: Shen et al. ran a two-layer network for vowel recognition on a silicon chip of 56 programmable MZIs, classifying 138 of 180 test cases correctly (76.7%) against 165 of 180 (91.7%) for the same network simulated on a computer (Nature Photon. 11, 441, 2017). The gap is the analogue error budget at work.
  • Errors compound with depth. Every path crosses about N MZIs, so splitter and phase errors accumulate. Fang et al. found that with beam-splitter errors above 1% and phase errors above 0.01 rad, a shallower, less expressive mesh (FFTNet) can beat the universal one (GridNet) (Opt. Express 27, 14009, 2019).
  • Errors can be calibrated out, within limits: Miller showed a self-configuring mesh tolerating fabricated split ratios anywhere from 85:15 to 15:85 (Optica 2, 747, 2015); Pai et al. showed how to initialise and optimise meshes so programming converges (Phys. Rev. Applied 11, 064044, 2019).
  • The cost: N2 phase shifters to hold, usually thermo-optic, each drawing static power (deck 02, slide 03), and footprint that limits N.
07

Interactive: a Mesh with Fabrication Errors

A rectangular mesh of N modes with random phases (a random matrix), run on random signed inputs twice: once ideal, once with Gaussian phase errors, splitter errors and loss in every MZI. The error is the RMS difference between the two outputs after removing the average loss; “equivalent bits” is −log2 of it. The error and loss values are inputs, not measurements; Fang et al.'s 0.01 rad and 1% are useful reference points (slide 06).

08

Incoherent and Wavelength-Multiplexed Engines

Put each input on its own wavelength, weight each wavelength with an attenuator, and sum all of them on one photodiode: a dot product with no interference and no phase to stabilise. Many photodiodes, each with its own row of weights, make a matrix–vector product (WDM).

Tait et al., silicon photonic weight banksMicroring resonators weight each wavelength; the circuit is shown to be isomorphic to a continuous-time neural network, with a simulated 294-fold speed-up over a conventional benchmarkSci. Rep. 7, 7430, 2017

  • Non-volatile weights (phase-change materials) hold a value with no power, unlike heaters (deck 02, slide 03); the price is few levels and slow, energetic rewriting.
  • Negative weights need two detectors and a subtraction (balanced detection), because power cannot be negative.
  • Many wavelengths need a comb or a laser array, and ring weights need tuning (multi-wavelength sources; thermal tuning).
09

Photoelectric Multiplication and the Photon Floor

Encode both the input and the weight in light, and interfere them on a balanced detector: the photocurrent is proportional to their product, and integrating over time sums many products. Hamerly et al. proposed this as a route to very large networks and derived a “standard quantum limit” set by detector shot noise, which “can be as low as 50 zJ/MAC”, below the Landauer limit for irreversible digital computing (Phys. Rev. X 9, 021032, 2019; a theoretical result).

  • These are optical energies only. They show how little light a multiplication needs when a network tolerates low precision; the electronics that generate, modulate, detect and digitise the light are not included, and in practice dominate (deck 02, slide 07).
  • The floor rises with precision: shot noise falls only as the square root of photon count, so each extra bit needs four times the photons (deck 02, slide 05).

The Nonlinearity Problem

A neural network needs a nonlinearity between its linear layers. Optics is linear at the intensities of these systems, so most designs detect the light, apply the nonlinearity electronically, and convert back: an optical–electrical–optical round trip per layer, with its conversion energy and latency.

WorkWhat it showedSource
Ashtiani, Geers and AflatouniAn on-chip photonic deep network classifying images directly from light in under 570 ps, “comparable with a single clock cycle” of digital platforms; above 93.8% (two-class) and 89.8% (four-class) on handwritten lettersNature 606, 501, 2022
Bandyopadhyay et al.A three-layer, six-neuron network computing linear and nonlinear functions on one chip with 410 ps latency; above 92% on a vowel taskNature Photon. 18, 1335, 2024
  • Optoelectronic nonlinearities (a detector driving a modulator) keep the signal in the optical domain at the cost of some electrical power per neuron; they make deep optical networks with low latency possible, so far at small sizes.
  • For large models the practical pattern is still optical linear layers inside a digital system that handles nonlinearities, normalisation and attention scores (deck 03, slide 04).
11

Training as Well as Inference

  • In-situ backpropagation: Pai et al. measured gradients in a silicon photonic network of MZI meshes by interfering forward- and backward-travelling light, and trained it to match digital simulations at above 94% test accuracy (Science 380, 398, 2023). The method is explained in in-situ backpropagation (the adjoint method).
  • Training around the hardware's imperfections: physics-aware training uses the real device in the forward pass and a model in the backward pass, closing the gap between simulation and hardware (physics-aware training; the reality gap).
  • Gradients need more precision than inference, which tilts training away from analogue hardware (gradient precision); the whole question is worked through in FOptTrn 02, Optical Backpropagation.

  • Why keep them apart: interconnect has a clear job, carrying bits with low energy and error, and is already deployed at scale. Optical computing replaces digital arithmetic with analogue arithmetic, which brings precision and conversion costs that interconnect does not have.
  • Where they meet: companies that started in optical computing have moved towards interconnect, and some offer both (deck 04).
13

What to Take Away

  • Optics computes linear operations for free by interference and propagation; it does not provide nonlinearity, memory or exactness, and every value must be converted in and out.
  • The families: free-space Fourier and diffractive (huge parallelism, slow weights), coherent MZI meshes (programmable, error-sensitive), incoherent WDM crossbars (robust, need many wavelengths), and photoelectric multiplication (both operands in light).
  • Results so far are small networks at modest accuracy (Shen: 76.7% against 91.7% in simulation) or vision tasks with light already in the optical domain (ACCEL, Taichi), with impressive optical energies (40 aJ, under one photon, per multiply) that exclude the electronics.
  • Errors compound in meshes (the interactive) and must be calibrated or trained around.
